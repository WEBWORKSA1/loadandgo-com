How this works

The calculator applies the two tests in the U.S. cargo securement rules for commercial vehicles and shows the larger answer.

Strength. The working load limits of all tie-downs added together must equal at least half the weight of the cargo. A strap that passes over the cargo and hooks to the vehicle on both sides counts for its full limit. A strap or chain that runs from one anchor on the vehicle to an anchor on the cargo counts for half.

Count. For cargo that is not blocked from sliding forward: one tie-down if it is 5 ft or shorter and 1,100 lb or lighter; two if it is 5 ft or shorter but heavier, or between 5 and 10 ft; and two plus one more for every further 10 ft, or part of 10 ft, beyond the first 10. Cargo blocked at the front needs one for every 10 ft or part of 10 ft.

Example: a 1,500 lb machine 8 ft long with 500 lb straps over the top needs 750 lb of total limit, so two straps by strength and two by length. Two is the minimum.

Questions people ask

Is working load limit the same as breaking strength?
No. Working load limit is the safe working figure, commonly one third of breaking strength. Packaging often prints the larger breaking number in big type. Plan with the limit.
Do these rules apply to my pickup or utility trailer?
The federal rule covers commercial motor vehicles. Every state also requires any vehicle's load to be secured so nothing can fall or shift, so the commercial minimums are a sensible floor for everyone.
What else matters besides the count?
Protect straps from sharp edges, pull opposite corners in opposite directions, and re-check tension after the first few miles, when loads settle.
