Less-than-truckload carriers price freight by weight, distance and class. Class is a number from 50 to 500 that stands for how hard a shipment is to carry. Dense, sturdy freight gets a low class and a low rate. Light, bulky or fragile freight gets a high one.

## Four characteristics, one that dominates

The National Motor Freight Classification assigns classes using four characteristics: density, handling, stowability and liability. Density, the weight of the shipment divided by the space it takes, has always mattered most. Since 2025 it matters more.

## What changed in 2025

The organisation that publishes the classification began a multi-year overhaul. The first phase took effect on 19 July 2025. Two changes affect everyday shippers:

- The standard density scale grew from 11 steps to 13. Classes 55 and 50 were added for freight of 35 and 50 pounds per cubic foot and above, so very dense freight now earns a lower class than before.
- Many commodities that used to have one fixed class, whatever they weighed, moved to the density scale. The same product can now land in different classes depending on how it is packed.

Items with real handling, stowability or liability concerns keep classes that reflect them. Further phases continue to consolidate and update listings.

## Working out density

1. Measure the shipment at its longest, widest and tallest points in inches, including the pallet and any overhang.
2. Multiply the three and divide by 1,728 to get cubic feet.
3. Divide the weight in pounds, pallet included, by the cubic feet.

A pallet 48 by 40 by 48 inches weighing 500 lb is 53.3 cubic feet and 9.4 pounds per cubic foot. On the 13-step scale that is class 100. Pack the same weight 36 inches high and density rises to 12.5, class 85.

## Why this is now a packaging question

Because class follows density, empty space costs money. Practical ways to move to a lower class:

- Use cartons that fit the product, without excess void fill.
- Stack pallets square and tight, with no overhang. Overhang is measured.
- Keep pallet height down where the product allows.
- Ship knocked-down or nested where you can.

## Avoiding reclassification charges

Carriers measure and weigh freight at their terminals, often with automated dimensioners. If their figures give a different class from the one on your bill of lading, they rebill at the higher rate and usually add an inspection fee.

To stay out of that: weigh the finished pallet on a scale, measure it after wrapping, write dimensions and the correct item number on the bill of lading, and keep a photo of each pallet with a tape measure in the frame.

## Check the official listing

A density calculation gives the class for items on the standard scale. The classification itself is the authority for your commodity, and your carrier or broker can confirm the item number and any special rules that apply.
