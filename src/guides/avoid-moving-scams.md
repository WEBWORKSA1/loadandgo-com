Most movers are honest. The dishonest ones follow a pattern that is easy to spot once you know it, and the United States gives you a free way to check any interstate mover before you sign.

## Look the mover up

Every company that moves household goods across state lines must be registered with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration and must have a USDOT number. The agency's Protect Your Move site has a search for registered movers. Enter the company name or number and confirm three things: the registration is active, it covers household goods, and the name and address match the company you are talking to. The same record shows complaint history.

Brokers, who sell the move and pass it to a carrier, must be registered too and must tell you they are brokers. For a move within one state, check with that state's regulator instead.

## Warning signs

- An estimate given by phone or email with no survey of your belongings, in person or by video.
- A price far below every other quote.
- A demand for a large deposit or full payment in cash before loading.
- No physical address, no registration number on the website, a phone answered with a generic "movers".
- Paperwork that is blank or incomplete when you are asked to sign.
- A rented truck with no company name arriving on moving day.
- A new, higher price announced after your goods are on the truck.

The last one is the classic hostage load: your belongings are held until you pay more than you agreed.

## What a proper estimate looks like

A legitimate interstate mover gives a written estimate based on a survey of what you are moving. It will be one of two kinds. A binding estimate fixes the price for the listed items and services. A non-binding estimate is the mover's best guess, with the final charge based on the actual weight; at delivery, the mover cannot require you to pay more than 110% of that estimate before unloading, with any balance billed afterwards.

The mover must also give you the federal booklet on your rights and responsibilities, and a bill of lading on moving day. Read both. Keep copies of everything.

## Protect yourself in practice

Get three written estimates from registered movers. Never sign blank documents. Pay by a method that leaves a record. Photograph valuable items and note existing damage on the inventory before it leaves. Carry documents, medicines, jewellery and irreplaceable things yourself.

## If it goes wrong

File a complaint with the federal regulator through Protect Your Move, and with your state consumer protection office. If goods are being held, say so clearly in the complaint; the agency treats hostage loads as a priority. Links are on our official resources page.
