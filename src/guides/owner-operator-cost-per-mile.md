A rate per mile means nothing until you know what a mile costs you. Operators who know their number turn down bad freight quickly and bargain from fact. Those who do not stay busy and wonder where the money went.

## Fixed costs

These bills arrive whether or not the truck turns a wheel: truck and trailer payments, insurance, permits and registrations, the electronic logging device subscription, parking, phone, accounting, load board fees. Add a month of them and divide by the miles you drive in a month, all miles, loaded and empty.

Fixed cost per mile falls as mileage rises. A truck with $4,000 of monthly fixed costs spends 50 cents a mile on them at 8,000 miles and 40 cents at 10,000. A week in the shop pushes the figure the other way.

## Variable costs

These rise with every mile.

- **Fuel.** Price per gallon divided by miles per gallon. At 6.5 miles per gallon, every dollar on the pump price adds about 15 cents a mile. Fuel is usually the largest single cost, and the fuel surcharge in a rate exists to cover its swings.
- **Maintenance and tires.** Set aside an amount per mile even in months when nothing breaks. The bill comes eventually.
- **Driver pay.** Pay yourself a wage per mile and count it as a cost. If the business only breaks even when you work for nothing, it is losing money.

Fixed plus variable is your cost per mile, and your break-even rate.

## Deadhead changes everything

Brokers quote on loaded miles. You drive all of them. A load paying $2.50 a mile for 400 loaded miles looks fine until you add 100 empty miles to reach the pickup. The $1,000 is spread over 500 miles: $2.00 a mile. If your cost is $1.90, the profit on that load is $50, not $240.

Always divide the total pay by total miles, including the empty miles to the pickup and, if the delivery is somewhere freight is scarce, the empty miles back out.

## Judging an offer

1. Add loaded and deadhead miles.
2. Multiply by your cost per mile. That is break-even for the trip.
3. Compare with the all-in rate, fuel surcharge included.
4. Weigh the rest: time waiting at shipper and receiver, tolls, where the load leaves you, and how quickly the broker pays.

A short load that pays well per mile can still lose a day to detention. Many operators therefore also track revenue per day.

## Reserves

Set money aside from every settlement for three things: taxes, a maintenance fund for major repairs such as an engine or after-treatment system, and downtime. A few cents a mile for each is common. Without reserves, one large repair can end a small business.

## Know your number, then review it

Recalculate each quarter with real figures from your statements. Fuel prices, insurance renewals and mileage all move. The calculator on this site fills in the current diesel average for your region, so you can see what a price swing does to your break-even before you take the next load.
