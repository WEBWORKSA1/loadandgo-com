Unsecured loads cause crashes, injuries and fines, and almost all of it is avoidable with a few straps used properly. The rules professional drivers follow are a good standard for anyone with a pickup or a trailer.

## Read the tag: working load limit

Every strap and chain made for cargo carries a working load limit, or WLL. It is the most the tie-down should hold in normal use and is commonly one third of its breaking strength. Packaging often shows the breaking strength in large print because it is the bigger number. Ignore it. Plan only with the WLL.

A tie-down is as strong as its weakest part. A 3,300 lb strap hooked to a 500 lb anchor point is a 500 lb tie-down. A strap with no legible tag should be treated as unrated and replaced.

## Test one: enough strength

Under the federal cargo securement rules for commercial vehicles, the working load limits of all the tie-downs on an item, added together, must come to at least half the item's weight. This sum is called the aggregate working load limit.

How a tie-down is attached changes what it contributes. One that goes over or around the cargo and attaches to the vehicle on both sides counts for its full WLL. One that runs from an anchor on the vehicle to an anchor on the cargo counts for half.

## Test two: enough tie-downs

The rules also set a minimum count by length, for cargo that is not blocked from moving forward:

| Cargo | Minimum tie-downs |
|---|---|
| 5 ft or shorter and 1,100 lb or lighter | 1 |
| 5 ft or shorter and heavier than 1,100 lb | 2 |
| Longer than 5 ft, up to 10 ft | 2 |
| Longer than 10 ft | 2, plus 1 for each further 10 ft or part of 10 ft |

Cargo butted against a bulkhead or other secured cargo needs one tie-down for every 10 ft or part of 10 ft.

You need whichever test gives the larger number. In practice, use at least two on anything: a single strap lets cargo pivot.

## Habits that matter as much as the count

- **Stop it sliding first.** Push cargo against the front of the bed or trailer. Blocking does more than tension.
- **Pull in opposing directions.** Straps at opposite corners, angled away from each other, resist movement every way.
- **Protect the webbing.** Sharp edges cut straps. Use corner guards or folded cardboard.
- **Mind the angle.** A strap running nearly flat across the top of a low load presses down very little. Steeper is better.
- **Tie off the tails.** Loose ends flap, fray and catch.
- **Re-check.** Stop after the first few miles and again at every break.

## Pickups and private trailers

The federal rules apply to commercial vehicles, but every state requires any load to be secured so that nothing can drop, shift or blow off, and several fine heavily for it. Loose material such as gravel, mulch and brush should also be covered with a tarp or net.
