LTL vs truckload vs partial: choosing how to ship freight

How less-than-truckload, partial and full truckload shipping differ in price, handling and transit time, and where the break points usually fall.

By the LoadandGo.com editors. Reviewed 26 Nov 2026. 2 min read.

Freight that is too big for a parcel carrier moves in one of three ways. The right one depends on how much trailer you need, how fast it must arrive and how well it tolerates handling.

Less than truckload

Your freight shares a trailer with other shippers' freight. A local truck collects it and takes it to a terminal, where it is sorted onto a line-haul trailer, moved between terminals, and delivered by another local truck.

  • Typical size: one to six pallets, roughly 150 to 10,000 lb.
  • Priced by: weight, freight class and distance, plus accessorial charges for extras such as a liftgate, residential delivery or an appointment.
  • Trade-offs: economical for small shipments, but the freight is handled several times, so it needs sturdy packaging, and transit times are estimates.

Full truckload

You hire the whole trailer. It is loaded at origin, sealed, and driven to the destination.

  • Typical size: 24 to 26 standard pallets loaded straight in a 53 ft dry van, up to about 45,000 lb.
  • Priced by: the lane and the market on the day, quoted as a flat rate or per mile. Class does not apply.
  • Trade-offs: fastest and gentlest, since nobody touches the freight in between. You pay for the trailer whether or not you fill it.

Partial truckload and volume LTL

Between the two sits a middle band. A partial truckload shares a trailer with one or two other shipments going the same way and usually travels without passing through terminals. Volume LTL is an LTL carrier's spot quote for a large shipment.

  • Typical size: about 6 to 18 pallets, or 5,000 to 25,000 lb.
  • Priced by: the trailer floor space used, in linear feet, and weight.
  • Trade-offs: less handling than LTL and cheaper than a full truck, but pickup and delivery dates are looser.

Where the break points fall

There is no fixed line, but these rules of thumb hold up:

  • Under six pallets and not urgent: LTL.
  • More than about 10 to 12 linear feet of trailer: ask for partial or volume quotes as well. Many LTL carriers apply a linear-foot or cubic-capacity rule above that size, which can raise the bill sharply.
  • More than half a trailer, or freight that is fragile, high value or on a deadline: price a full truckload. The premium is often smaller than expected.

Does it weigh out or cube out?

A trailer has two limits, floor space and weight, and every load hits one of them first. Twenty-six pallets at 1,800 lb each would weigh 46,800 lb, over what most rigs can legally carry, so that load weighs out at about 25. Twenty-six pallets of pillows weigh almost nothing and cube out. Knowing which limit you will hit tells you whether to bargain on weight or on space.

What to have ready for any quote

Pickup and delivery postcodes, pallet count, dimensions and weight of each pallet, whether pallets can be stacked, the commodity, and what each end has: a dock, a forklift, or neither.
