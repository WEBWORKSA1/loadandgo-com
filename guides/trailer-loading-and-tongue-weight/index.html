How to load a trailer: the 60/40 rule and tongue weight

Why trailers sway, how to place cargo so they do not, how to measure tongue weight and what to do if sway starts.

By the LoadandGo.com editors. Reviewed 26 Nov 2026. 2 min read.

A trailer that is loaded tail-heavy can feel fine at 40 miles per hour and become uncontrollable at 60. The cause is nearly always the same, and so is the cure: put more of the weight ahead of the axle.

The 60/40 rule

Place about 60% of the cargo weight in the front half of the trailer, ahead of the axle, and 40% behind. Heavy items go on the floor, centred side to side, just forward of the axle. Lighter things fill the rear.

This is the guidance trailer rental companies teach, and demonstration videos with model trailers on a treadmill show why. With weight forward, a nudge from a gust or a passing truck dies out. With weight at the back, the same nudge grows with each swing.

Tongue weight: the number behind the rule

Tongue weight is the downward force the trailer puts on the hitch ball. For a bumper-pull trailer the target is 10 to 15% of the loaded trailer's weight. For a fifth wheel or gooseneck, where the hitch sits over the truck's axle, the target is higher, about 15 to 25%.

Too little tongue weight and the trailer steers the truck. Too much and the back of the truck sags, the front wheels lighten, and steering and braking suffer. Too much also uses up payload, because the truck carries that weight.

Measuring it

  • Tongue weight scale. A purpose-made scale sits under the coupler and reads directly.
  • Public scale. Weigh the truck alone, then with the trailer coupled but only the truck's wheels on the scale. The difference is the tongue weight.
  • Bathroom scale and lever. For light trailers, a beam and two supports let a household scale read a fraction of the load. Instructions are widely published; check the arithmetic twice.

If the figure is low, move cargo forward. If it is high, move some back, but never behind the point where the figure drops below 10%.

Other things that keep a trailer straight

  • Level the trailer. A nose-high trailer shifts weight rearward.
  • Inflate trailer tires to the pressure on the trailer's label, checked cold.
  • Secure the cargo. A load that slides back on a hill changes the balance while you drive.
  • Cross the safety chains under the coupler and connect the lights and breakaway cable.
  • Slow down. Many trailer tires are rated for 65 miles per hour, and sway grows with speed.

If sway starts

Hold the wheel straight. Ease off the accelerator. Do not brake hard and do not try to steer out of it. If the trailer has electric brakes, apply them gently by hand with the controller. Once the rig settles, stop somewhere safe and move weight forward before continuing.
